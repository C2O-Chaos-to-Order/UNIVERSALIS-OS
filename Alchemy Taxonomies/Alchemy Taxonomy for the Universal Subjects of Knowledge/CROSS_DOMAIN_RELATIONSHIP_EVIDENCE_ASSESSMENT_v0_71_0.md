This assessment continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences programme using the next three pairs in the v0.70.0 regenerated remaining inventory:

- Immunology ↔ Health Sciences
- Virology ↔ Health Sciences

The assessment uses exact unordered subject-pair matching against the assessment-table rows in all 70 preceding snapshots. A candidate RD-P requires more than general relevance: it must identify a contribution from each subject, a stable cross-domain research or practice boundary, and an interface that is not merely a restatement of an already-assessed specialist pair. A root-level candidate must also explain why its integrated scope adds value beyond the existing specialist pairings without claiming that every Health Sciences activity depends on the Life Sciences subject.

The three recommendations are bounded as follows:

- **Anatomy ↔ Health Sciences:** anatomical structure, spatial relationships, morphological variation, and anatomical measurement that support multiple health-science settings, including imaging interpretation, clinical and procedural reasoning, surgical planning, anatomical phenotyping, and translational research.
- **Immunology ↔ Health Sciences:** immune-system mechanisms, cells, pathways, responses, and measurement that inform multiple health-science settings, including infectious disease, immunization, autoimmunity, allergy, cancer, transplantation, disease prevention, and selected immune-based interventions.
- **Virology ↔ Health Sciences:** viral structure, replication, pathogenesis, host interaction, detection, and characterization that inform multiple health-science settings, including diagnosis, prevention, vaccination, antiviral development, outbreak and genomic surveillance, and health-system preparedness.

Research relevance is not clinical validation. A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. It does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, verification, or owner acceptance. No canonical change is made by this report.

### LSHS-267 — Anatomy ↔ Health Sciences

Anatomy contributes knowledge of the structure, organization, spatial relationships, development, and variation of the human body across regional, systemic, gross, microscopic, and clinically relevant scales. Health Sciences contributes the diagnostic, procedural, therapeutic, preventive, functional, and population-health questions through which anatomical evidence is interpreted and applied.

Three contemporary sources establish a cross-cutting interface. A 2026 review of clinical-anatomy education identifies diagnostic imaging, procedural practice, surgery, simulation, and patient safety as major points of integration, while cautioning that educational novelty does not by itself demonstrate durable transfer into clinical performance. A 2026 review tracing anatomy through radiology into imaging science describes how anatomical concepts inform image-derived measurements, quantitative phenotypes, imaging biomarkers, and therapeutic evidence; it emphasizes that a measured image feature becomes a biomarker only in a defined context of use, and that association alone is insufficient to qualify a surrogate endpoint. A 2026 whole-body CT segmentation resource harmonizes anatomical labels across more than 100 imaging centres and 22,022 CT volumes, annotating 167 structures and demonstrating a substantial research infrastructure for anatomical characterization and quantitative imaging. Together, these sources show that anatomical knowledge and methods contribute across multiple health-science contexts rather than only to a single clinical specialty. [S654–S656]

The relationship is distinct from Anatomy ↔ Medicine (LSHS-002), Occupational Therapy (LSHS-086), Physical Therapy (LSHS-087), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-088), Surgery (LSHS-103), Pharmacology (LSHS-116), Nursing (LSHS-147), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-148), Dentistry (LSHS-149), Nutrition (LSHS-172), Mental Health (LSHS-178), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-187), Public Health (LSHS-194), and Epidemiology (LSHS-202). These records address specific clinical, procedural, professional, functional, or population-research settings. LSHS-267 concerns the cross-cutting anatomical-science contribution across multiple Health Sciences settings, including the interpretation and validation of structural evidence, and does not duplicate or replace those narrower applications.

**Candidate RD-P:** human body structure and organization; regional, systemic, gross, and microscopic anatomy; anatomical relationships, landmarks, and clinically relevant variation; morphology and structural phenotypes; anatomical imaging and quantitative structural characterization / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and practice involving diagnostic and procedural reasoning, imaging interpretation, surgical and intervention planning, patient-specific anatomical assessment, anatomical phenotyping, functional and population research, and evaluation of structure-related health outcomes. Anatomy contributes the structural models, spatial relationships, descriptive systems, and morphological measurement principles; Health Sciences contributes clinical and population-health questions, patient and disease contexts, diagnostic and procedural requirements, safety and outcome criteria, and health-service settings. The relationship is reciprocal: health-science needs and observations guide anatomical investigation, while validated anatomical knowledge informs selected assessment, research, planning, and intervention. This does not imply that anatomical measurements automatically establish diagnosis, that an imaging feature is a validated biomarker or surrogate endpoint, or that a general anatomical contribution reverses any specialist-pair decision.**

### LSHS-268 — Immunology ↔ Health Sciences

Immunology contributes knowledge of immune-system organization and function, including innate and adaptive responses, immune-cell populations, antibodies, cytokines, antigen recognition, immune regulation, tolerance, inflammation, and immune-mediated disease mechanisms. Health Sciences contributes the clinical and population-health questions, disease and patient contexts, preventive priorities, treatment pathways, safety requirements, and outcomes through which immune evidence is evaluated and applied.

NIH's October 2026 statement establishing a Vaccinology and Immunology Center describes a planned multidisciplinary research programme bringing together patients, physician-scientists, and laboratory researchers to investigate health problems reported after vaccination or infection, examine possible pathophysiology and risk factors, and inform treatment research and vaccine safety. The announcement states that the study protocol was still being finalized and that launch was anticipated before the end of 2026; it is evidence of a translational research interface, not proof of any particular causal mechanism or adverse-event hypothesis. NIH's August 2026 research priority on immune mechanisms for disease prevention explicitly encourages investigation of immune-based mechanisms across infectious disease, cancer, autoimmune conditions, and allergic disease, including physical barriers, innate and adaptive responses, and immune regulation. A 2026 review of immuno-AI describes the gap between promising predictive performance and clinical adoption, identifying validation, interpretability, and regulatory integration as unresolved constraints. These sources demonstrate research, prevention, and translational links across multiple Health Sciences domains while requiring application-specific validation. [S657–S659]

The relationship is distinct from Immunology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-011), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-035), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-036), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-046), Public Health (LSHS-047), Dentistry (LSHS-053), Nursing (LSHS-054), Pharmacology (LSHS-104), Surgery (LSHS-105), Nutrition (LSHS-106), Epidemiology (LSHS-107), Mental Health (LSHS-141), Physical Therapy (LSHS-142), and Occupational Therapy (LSHS-143). Those records concern narrower clinical, professional, therapeutic, or population applications. In particular, the earlier Immunology ↔ Occupational Therapy assessment (LSHS-143) was **not promoted** because the evidence did not establish a distinct immunology-specific contribution to occupational-therapy practice beyond adjacent disease management and self-management. LSHS-268 does not reverse that decision. It concerns the broader immunological research and health-response interface across multiple Health Sciences settings and does not imply that every health condition is immune-mediated or that immune biomarkers are automatically clinically actionable.

**Candidate RD-P:** immune-system structure and function; innate and adaptive immunity; immune cells, antibodies, cytokines, antigen recognition, and immune signalling; immune regulation, tolerance, inflammation, and immunopathology; immunological assays, immune profiling, and response measurement / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and practice involving infectious-disease investigation, immunization and vaccine research, immune-mediated and allergic disease, autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, cancer and transplant immunology, disease-prevention research, and selected immune-based diagnostic or therapeutic evaluation. Immunology contributes immune mechanisms, measurements, biological models, and interpretation of immune responses; Health Sciences contributes patient and population contexts, disease phenotypes, clinical questions, prevention and treatment pathways, safety and efficacy standards, and outcome validation. The relationship is reciprocal: health-science needs and observations guide immunological research, while validated immune evidence informs selected health-science research and applications. This does not establish causation from immune association, generalize a biomarker across diseases, or imply that emerging immune-based methods are ready for routine care.**

### LSHS-269 — Virology ↔ Health Sciences

Virology contributes knowledge of viruses and viral systems, including viral structure, replication, genetics, evolution, pathogenesis, host-cell interaction, immune evasion, transmission, detection, and antiviral susceptibility. Health Sciences contributes the clinical and population-health contexts, disease phenotypes, diagnostic and preventive requirements, therapeutic and vaccine evaluation, surveillance systems, and health-service responses through which virological evidence is investigated and applied.

NIH's Virology research overview describes basic research into viral particles and host interaction alongside work on viral disease pathogenesis, prevention, diagnostics, antiviral therapies, and vaccines. WHO's 2026 Global Hepatitis Report assesses hepatitis B and C burden and progress in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, showing how virological disease knowledge connects with population programmes and health-service delivery. WHO's 2026–2030 Hub for Pandemic and Epidemic Intelligence strategy emphasizes strengthened surveillance, faster threat detection, applied analytics, pathogen surveillance, and trusted digital foundations. Previously registered CDC influenza genomic-sequencing infrastructure (S104) and a 2026 review of broader-spectrum direct-acting antivirals against RNA viruses (S238) provide additional evidence of the links between viral characterization, public-health preparedness, and therapeutic development. These sources establish a cross-cutting interface, but the properties, diagnostic pathways, available preventives, and treatment options remain virus- and indication-specific. [S660–S662; previously registered S104 and S238]

The relationship is distinct from Epidemiology ↔ Virology (LSHS-004), Virology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-010), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-015), Public Health (LSHS-043), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-044), Nursing (LSHS-045), Pharmacology (LSHS-109), Dentistry (LSHS-150), Surgery (LSHS-151), Nutrition (LSHS-152), Physical Therapy (LSHS-153), Occupational Therapy (LSHS-154), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-155), and Mental Health (LSHS-156). Those records concern specific clinical, population, professional, therapeutic, or post-infectious applications. The reverse-ordered Epidemiology ↔ Virology entry is a distinct specialist pair and is not an exact match for Virology ↔ Health Sciences. LSHS-269 concerns the cross-cutting virological contribution across multiple Health Sciences settings and does not collapse Virology into infectious-disease medicine, epidemiology, public health, or any single specialty.

**Candidate RD-P:** viral structure, taxonomy, genetics, replication, and evolution; viral pathogenesis and host–virus interactions; viral immunology and transmission; viral detection, characterization, and genomic analysis; and antiviral susceptibility and resistance mechanisms / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and practice involving viral diagnosis, disease-mechanism investigation, vaccine and antiviral development and evaluation, infection prevention, clinical and population surveillance, outbreak and pandemic preparedness, and assessment of viral contributions to health and disease. Virology contributes pathogen-specific biological knowledge, methods, and interpretation; Health Sciences contributes clinical and population-health questions, patient and disease phenotypes, diagnostic and prevention pathways, therapeutic and vaccine evaluation, public-health systems, and health outcomes. The relationship is reciprocal: health-science needs and surveillance findings direct virological investigation, while validated virological evidence informs selected clinical, preventive, therapeutic, and population-health research and response. This does not imply that all viruses have the same clinical significance, that detection establishes causation or disease severity, or that research-stage antivirals and vaccines are clinically effective.**
