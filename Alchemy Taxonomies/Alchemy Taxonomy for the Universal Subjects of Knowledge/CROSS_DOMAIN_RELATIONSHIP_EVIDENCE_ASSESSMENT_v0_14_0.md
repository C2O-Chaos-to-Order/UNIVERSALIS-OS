## 2. Method and important terminology boundaries

Each pair is assessed for a specific interface between the two canonical subject scopes. A named interdisciplinary subfield is useful evidence of an interface, but it does not erase the identities of its contributing disciplines or establish that either discipline is contained within the other.

Important boundaries:
- **Evolutionary Biology is not synonymous with Epidemiology.** Evolutionary models explain variation, adaptation, selection, lineage turnover, and pathogen evolution. Epidemiology studies population distribution, determinants, transmission, and control. Phylodynamics explicitly combines evolutionary and epidemiological models, but it depends on assumptions about the relationship between pathogen genetic change and transmission dynamics.
- **Evolutionary Biology is not synonymous with Public Health.** Evolutionary reasoning can inform public-health responses to antimicrobial resistance and other evolving biological threats. The assessed interface is limited to how evolutionary processes inform population-level prevention, surveillance, and intervention—not a claim that all public-health work is evolutionary biology.
- **Genetics is not synonymous with Epidemiology.** Genetic epidemiology studies genetic and familial determinants of disease-related traits in populations. It combines population-based epidemiological questions with genetic analysis; it is a bounded interdisciplinary field, not a reclassification of all genetics or epidemiology.
- **Genetics is not synonymous with Public Health.** Public-health genomics applies genetic knowledge to population health, prevention, and health-equity objectives. A genetic association, risk score, or test does not automatically justify population screening or public-health action; validity, utility, implementation, consent, access, and equity remain relevant.
- **Molecular Biology is not synonymous with Epidemiology.** Molecular epidemiology uses molecular and biological markers or methods in population-based study of disease distribution and determinants. This batch focuses on molecular characterization and molecular methods, not the previously screened Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology pair, which addresses a different subject scope.
- **Molecular Biology is not synonymous with Public Health.** Molecular detection, assays, and pathogen genomic characterization can supply evidence to public-health surveillance and response systems. The molecular result must be interpreted alongside epidemiological context, metadata, quality assurance, and public-health decision processes; measurement alone is not population-level benefit.
- The distinction between **host genetic variation**, **pathogen molecular characterization**, and **pathogen evolutionary dynamics** is maintained. They may be used together in the same investigation, but they answer different questions and do not become interchangeable.
- A candidate RD-P is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. Evidence of an interface does not establish universal dependency, containment, hierarchy, or proof that a particular public-health intervention is effective.
- Evidence grades describe the strength and directness of the subject-pair interface, not the certainty that every application or intervention produces a clinical or population benefit.

## 4. Duplicate, direction, and scope cross-check

The six pairs were compared as unordered subject pairs against the assessment tables in v0.1.0 through v0.13.0. None is an exact duplicate. They receive the next sequential identifiers, LSHS-089 through LSHS-094.

Related but distinct assessments already in the ledger include:
- Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-003);
- Virology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-004);
- Genetics ↔ Medicine (LSHS-017);
- Genetics ↔ Pharmacology (LSHS-020);
- Genetics ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-022);
- Molecular Biology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-018);
- Molecular Biology ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-023);
- Microbiology and Virology connections with Public Health (LSHS-040 and LSHS-043);
- Immunology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-047).

These do not duplicate the new pairings. The distinction is substantive:
- LSHS-089 examines pathogen evolution and population transmission models, rather than the general organism-focused Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology pairing.
- LSHS-090 examines evolutionary mechanisms as inputs to public-health interventions, particularly antimicrobial resistance; it is not the same as the Microbiology/Virology ↔ Public Health surveillance interfaces.
- LSHS-091 concerns inherited host variation, gene–environment interaction, and population disease risk; it is distinct from Genetics ↔ Medicine and Genetics ↔ Preventive Medicine.
- LSHS-092 concerns the population-health translation of genetic knowledge and inherited-risk programmes, not individual clinical diagnosis or treatment alone.
- LSHS-093 concerns molecular techniques and markers used in population-based investigation; it does not subsume Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology.
- LSHS-094 concerns how molecular results become actionable public-health surveillance and response inputs. It is distinct from molecular epidemiological analysis because it focuses on the wider public-health system and its programme decisions.

Direction is recorded as reciprocal at the bounded interface where both subject scopes contribute to a combined research or operational process. This is not a claim of equal contribution in every application, nor a dependency, enablement, or hierarchy edge.
