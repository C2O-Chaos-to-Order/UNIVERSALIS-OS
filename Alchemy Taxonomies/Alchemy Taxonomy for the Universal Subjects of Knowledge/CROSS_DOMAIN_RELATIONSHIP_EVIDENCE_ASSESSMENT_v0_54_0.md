## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. This subgroup examines evolutionary principles applied to rehabilitation research, an apparent evolutionary connection in emergency medicine that resolves to the history of the specialty rather than biological evolution, and blue-space/nature-based occupational therapy evaluated against the disciplinary scope of Marine Biology.

The assessment distinguishes:

- **Evolutionary Biology ↔ Rehabilitation Science:** comparative and evolutionary neuroscience research identifies conserved interlimb locomotor-control mechanisms across humans and other animals and examines how those mechanisms may inform neurorehabilitation after stroke, spinal-cord injury, and traumatic brain injury. Evolutionary Biology contributes explanations of evolutionary conservation, ancestry, and comparative biological mechanisms; Rehabilitation Science contributes translational functional-recovery questions, rehabilitation targets, and outcome contexts. The interface is bounded to locomotor and neuromechanical rehabilitation research. The reviewed literature is mechanistic and translational; it does not establish a universally effective intervention or show that evolutionary analysis is required for all rehabilitation.
- **Evolutionary Biology ↔ Emergency Medicine:** a literature search surfaced publications using phrases such as “evolutionary perspective” and “evolution of emergency medicine.” On inspection, these address the historical development, organisation, and future direction of Emergency Medicine as a medical specialty and emergency-care systems. They do not apply biological evolutionary theory, phylogenetic comparison, natural selection, adaptation, or evolutionary mechanisms to acute-care problems. The lexical use of “evolution” is not evidence of a disciplinary interface with Evolutionary Biology. This pair is not promoted.
- **Marine Biology ↔ Occupational Therapy:** occupational-therapy literature describes engagement with nature, outdoor occupations, coastal or aquatic settings, and blue-space activities as potential contexts for wellbeing, participation, and meaningful occupation. These are legitimate occupational-therapy applications. The reviewed sources do not demonstrate that marine organisms, marine ecosystems, marine physiology, or Marine Biology methods materially shape assessment or intervention design. A coastal or aquatic setting alone does not establish a Marine Biology contribution. This pair is not promoted.

A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. A candidate does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, or verification. A non-promotion is specific to the scope and evidence assessed in this pass; it does not prove that no narrower future interface could be supported. No canonical change is made by this report.

- **Emergency Medicine:** Emergency Medicine has previously been assessed with other Life Sciences subjects, including Microbiology (LSHS-041), Virology (LSHS-044), Immunology (LSHS-035), Physiology (LSHS-037), Biotechnology (LSHS-207), Molecular Biology (LSHS-164), and others. - **Marine Biology:** Marine Biology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-060), Public Health (LSHS-061), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-062), Nutrition (LSHS-066), Epidemiology (LSHS-119), Pharmacology (LSHS-121), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-162), Mental Health (LSHS-179), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-191; not promoted), Nursing (LSHS-192), Dentistry (LSHS-206; not promoted), Surgery (LSHS-212; not promoted), and Physical Therapy (LSHS-215; not promoted) concern other exact pairs. LSHS-218 examines Occupational Therapy and nature/blue-space engagement. It is not a duplicate of Marine Biology ↔ Physical Therapy: the occupational-therapy literature concerns meaningful occupation and participation, while the previous physical-therapy pair concerned aquatic rehabilitation. Neither water nor a coastal setting alone establishes a Marine Biology contribution.

Direction and scope:

- LSHS-216 is reciprocal within translational research: Evolutionary Biology contributes comparative and evolutionary explanations of conserved locomotor mechanisms; Rehabilitation Science contributes functional-recovery questions, assessment, intervention research, and outcome evaluation.
- LSHS-217 has no promoted direction or subtype. The reviewed “evolution” sources concern the development of the specialty and emergency-care systems, not biological evolution.
- LSHS-218 has no promoted direction or subtype. Nature-based and blue-space occupational therapy can be legitimate intervention contexts without a necessary contribution from Marine Biology.

Important boundaries:

- Evolutionary conservation and comparative animal research can generate hypotheses for human neurorehabilitation, but animal findings do not directly prescribe human treatment or prove clinical effectiveness.
- “Evolution” used to describe the historical development of a medical specialty is not equivalent to Evolutionary Biology.
- Blue-space, coastal, and outdoor occupational activities are not Marine Biology merely because they occur near or in the sea. A distinct Marine Biology relationship requires a material contribution from marine organisms, ecosystems, physiology, or disciplinary methods.

None of these decisions creates a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim. This assessment does not infer clinical efficacy from mechanistic reviews, conflate institutional history with biological evolution, or classify natural aquatic settings as Marine Biology.
