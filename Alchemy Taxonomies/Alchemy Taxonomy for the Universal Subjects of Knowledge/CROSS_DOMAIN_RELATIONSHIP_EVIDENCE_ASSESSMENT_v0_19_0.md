## 2. Method and important terminology boundaries

Each pair is assessed for a specific interface between Mycology and the relevant Health Sciences subject. The assessment distinguishes fungal biology and organism-level characteristics from population-level measurement, public-health system action, and operative clinical management.

Important boundaries:
- **Mycology is not synonymous with Epidemiology.** Mycology studies fungi, their taxonomy, morphology, physiology, ecology, pathogenicity, host interactions, and biological variation. Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health conditions in populations. Their interface includes fungal disease incidence, prevalence, geographic distribution, risk factors, outbreak investigation, surveillance, and the interpretation of laboratory and clinical case data.
- **Fungal disease burden estimates are not equivalent to complete population surveillance.** Fungal diseases are frequently underdiagnosed or underreported, and reporting obligations differ by jurisdiction and pathogen. Some estimates rely on claims, hospital records, death certificates, or electronic health-record algorithms. These sources have different case definitions, denominators, ascertainment limitations, and geographic precision.
- **Mycology is not synonymous with Public Health.** Mycology supplies knowledge of fungal species, pathogenicity, environmental reservoirs, antifungal resistance, and diagnostic needs. Public Health uses this evidence to prioritize risks, strengthen laboratory capacity and surveillance, design prevention and awareness efforts, guide antifungal stewardship, and coordinate health-system and policy responses. The existence of fungal disease does not by itself prove the effectiveness of any particular public-health programme.
- **Epidemiology and Public Health remain distinct.** Epidemiology contributes methods to measure and explain population patterns and determinants. Public Health has a broader programme, policy, prevention, preparedness, service, and systems remit. The same fungal burden evidence may inform both, but the two relationships are not interchangeable.
- **Mycology is not synonymous with Surgery.** Surgery is not required for every fungal infection. A specific interface exists in selected invasive fungal infections—particularly mucormycosis and certain localized mould infections—where surgical consultation, debridement, resection, or source control may be part of multidisciplinary treatment alongside antifungal therapy and management of host risk factors.
- **Operative management is organism-, site-, and patient-specific.** The feasibility and extent of surgery depend on infection site, tissue invasion, vascular involvement, disease progression, patient stability, comorbidities, immune status, and the possibility of achieving meaningful source control. Surgical debridement guidance for invasive mould disease must not be generalized to superficial mycoses or all invasive fungal disease.
- **Specimen and tissue findings matter.** Mycological identification, histopathology, culture, and, where appropriate, molecular methods can inform diagnosis and the extent of tissue involvement. Surgical specimens and operative observations can in turn support mycological characterization and treatment reassessment.
- A candidate RD-P is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. Evidence of an interface does not establish universal dependency, containment, hierarchy, or guaranteed clinical or population benefit.
- Evidence grades describe the strength and directness of the bounded subject-pair interface, not the certainty that every fungal surveillance estimate is complete or that every invasive fungal infection benefits from surgery.

These do not duplicate the three new Mycology relationships. The distinction is substantive:
- LSHS-111 examines the use of mycological knowledge in population incidence, distribution, determinants, and surveillance analysis. It is not the previously assessed Mycology ↔ Medicine or Mycology ↔ Preventive Medicine relationship.
- LSHS-112 concerns public-health prioritization, surveillance infrastructure, laboratory capacity, prevention, policy, and coordinated response. It is distinct from Epidemiology's narrower measurement and analysis of population patterns and from the previously assessed Microbiology/Virology ↔ Public Health relationships.
- LSHS-113 concerns operative source control in selected invasive fungal infections. It is distinct from Mycology ↔ Medicine and Mycology ↔ Emergency Medicine because the focus is on surgical evaluation, debridement, resection, and operative findings.

Direction is recorded as reciprocal at the bounded interface where both subject scopes contribute, but this is not a claim of equal contribution in every setting:
- LSHS-111: mycological knowledge informs case classification and disease-specific interpretation; epidemiological findings identify population patterns, geographic shifts, and unanswered organism-level questions.
- LSHS-112: mycology supplies pathogen, diagnostic, and resistance knowledge; public-health systems translate it into priorities, surveillance, preparedness, prevention, and resource decisions.
- LSHS-113: mycology informs diagnosis and organism-specific assessment; surgery provides procedural source control and tissue findings that can support ongoing mycological evaluation.

None of these proposals is a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim.
