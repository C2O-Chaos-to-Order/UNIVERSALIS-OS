## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. It assesses three previously unassessed subject pairs at the intersection of biological stress response, mental health, and environmental exposure:

- Physiology ↔ Mental Health: autonomic, cardiovascular, hemodynamic, and neuroendocrine responses to psychological stress; physiological stress reactivity and recovery; and the role and limitations of physiological measures in mental-health research.
- Anatomy ↔ Mental Health: structural neuroanatomy and brain morphology, including regional gray-matter volume, cortical thickness and surface area, and anatomical variation investigated in relation to depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and other psychiatric conditions.
- Marine Biology ↔ Mental Health: considered through the literature on coastal and other blue-space exposure and psychological outcomes. The pair is not promoted because the reviewed blue-space studies generally measure exposure to water environments, their visual or spatial characteristics, or perceived quality—not marine organisms, marine community composition, ecological processes, or other variables specific to Marine Biology.

The first two pairs represent different kinds of biological contribution:

- Physiology ↔ Mental Health concerns how the body responds to psychological stress and regulates systems involved in homeostasis. Relevant measures include cardiac and hemodynamic reactivity, autonomic regulation, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity, cortisol and related neuroendocrine markers, and physiological recovery. These measures can contribute to research on stress and mental-health conditions, but no single physiological measure is a universal or standalone diagnostic test.
- Anatomy ↔ Mental Health concerns structural organization and morphology rather than physiological function. Structural neuroimaging studies quantify anatomical characteristics and regional variation in the brain in relation to mental-health phenotypes. This is distinct in emphasis from the existing Neuroscience ↔ Mental Health relationship (LSHS-005), which is broader and includes neural processes, function, circuits, and mechanisms. The proposed anatomical interface is limited to structural neuroanatomy and morphometric interpretation. Structural associations do not establish causal direction, individual diagnosis, or a diagnostic biomarker.
- Marine Biology ↔ Mental Health is not promoted. Blue-space exposure is an active environmental-psychology and public-health research area, and coastal settings are frequently studied. However, water exposure, proximity to a coast, or viewing water does not itself constitute a Marine Biology variable. The reviewed literature does not establish a sufficiently distinct Marine Biology contribution to mental-health assessment, intervention, or research. Reconsideration would require direct measurement of marine biodiversity, organismal exposure, marine ecological processes, or another clearly marine-biological variable linked to defined mental-health outcomes.

The evidence for Physiology ↔ Mental Health is substantive but nuanced. A 2026 meta-analysis identified 126 studies of physiological responses to standardized acute psychological stress and depression. It found a small blunting of overall physiological reactivity, driven mainly by cardiac and hemodynamic measures; it did not find statistically significant overall associations for heart-rate variability or neuroendocrine reactivity, and it found no significant association for reactivity to the cold-pressor task. The findings support physiological research relevance but do not validate a universal depression biomarker. A further 2026 review maps HPA-axis, autonomic, immune, genetic, and multi-omic stress measures and emphasizes differences between acute and chronic stress, sampling and sensor limitations, and the need for careful interpretation.

The evidence for Anatomy ↔ Mental Health includes large-scale structural neuroimaging syntheses. A meta-analysis of 57,717 subjects combined 1,021 voxel-based morphometry experiments and found diagnosis-selective gray-matter findings for bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, but not a universal structural signature across all psychiatric conditions. A separate study using six population datasets and 23,417 participants found associations between depression symptoms and gray-matter volume or cortical surface area in frontal, anterior cingulate, and insular regions, while also noting the history of inconsistent results in the literature. These findings support a research interface involving anatomical structure and mental-health phenotypes, not clinical diagnosis from anatomy alone.

The blue-space evidence supports the Marine Biology non-promotion. A 2026 systematic evidence map included 139 papers examining types and characteristics of blue spaces and mental-health outcomes. Coastal environments were the most frequently studied type, but only six papers directly compared different blue-space types; study designs, exposure descriptions, populations, and outcomes were heterogeneous. A separate systematic review of depression-related outcomes included eight observational studies involving 35,797 adults but found exposure and outcome measures too heterogeneous to support a common quantitative synthesis. Neither review establishes that marine biological variables are the explanatory or intervention mechanism. The non-promotion is therefore a boundary on disciplinary attribution, not a denial that coastal and marine environments may be relevant to human wellbeing.

Previously assessed and related pairs remain distinct:

- Neuroscience ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-005) is an existing relationship. LSHS-178 is limited to structural anatomy and brain morphometry, rather than the broader neural processes, function, circuits, and mechanisms covered by Neuroscience. The two interfaces are related but are not treated as interchangeable.
- Evolutionary Biology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-140) concerns evolutionary explanations relevant to mental-health traits and conditions; Physiology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-177) concerns body-system regulation and physiological stress responses. These are distinct explanatory levels.
- Immunology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-141), Virology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-156), Microbiology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-157), and Mycology ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-158) concern immune, viral, microbial, and fungal interfaces. They are not duplicates of whole-body stress physiology or structural neuroanatomy.

- Marine Biology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-060), Marine Biology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-061), Marine Biology ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-062), Marine Biology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-066), Marine Biology ↔ Pharmacology (LSHS-121), Marine Biology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-119), and Marine Biology ↔ Emergency Medicine (LSHS-162) concern other health-science interfaces. None establishes a prior Marine Biology ↔ Mental Health assessment.

Direction and scope:

- LSHS-177 is reciprocal within a bounded research interface: Physiology contributes mechanisms and measures of bodily stress response; Mental Health contributes clinical and psychological phenotypes and interprets their significance. Neither discipline alone determines the other's findings.
- LSHS-178 is reciprocal within a bounded structural research interface: Anatomy contributes structural description and localization; Mental Health defines the clinical and psychological phenotypes whose structural correlates are investigated. Structural associations do not establish diagnostic utility or causality.
- LSHS-179 has no promoted direction or subtype. The blue-space evidence does not justify a general marine-biological-to-mental-health or mental-health-to-marine-biological recommendation.

None of these decisions creates a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim. The assessment does not treat a physiological measure as a standalone psychiatric diagnosis, structural brain morphology as proof of a disorder or its cause, or coastal water exposure as proof of a Marine Biology contribution.
