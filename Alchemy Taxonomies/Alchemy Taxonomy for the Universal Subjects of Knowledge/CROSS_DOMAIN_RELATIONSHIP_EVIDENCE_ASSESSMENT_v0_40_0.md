## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. It assesses three previously unassessed interfaces involving living systems and mental health:

- Ecology ↔ Mental Health: ecological characteristics of environments, biodiversity-related exposure, habitat and environmental quality, and their associations with psychological well-being, stress, depression, and anxiety.
- Zoology ↔ Mental Health: animal behaviour, cognition, species-specific social behaviour, ethology, and animal welfare considerations in animal-assisted interventions for people with mental-health needs.
- Botany ↔ Mental Health: assessed as a potential interface through horticultural therapy, gardening, and other plant-based activities. The interventions show promising results in some reviews, but the evidence reviewed does not establish a sufficiently distinct Botany-specific contribution to mental-health assessment or treatment; the pair is not promoted in this pass.

These interfaces are related but distinct:

- Ecology ↔ Mental Health concerns the ecological character and biodiversity of environments in which people live and interact. It is not equivalent to a generic exposure to greenery, and it does not establish that ecological biodiversity independently causes or treats a psychiatric disorder.
- Zoology ↔ Mental Health concerns the scientific understanding of animals participating in therapeutic contexts: species and individual suitability, behavioural repertoire, social and sensory capabilities, stress indicators, welfare, and safe interaction. Mental Health evaluates human psychological outcomes, suitability, safeguards, and intervention context. Animal welfare is a co-equal consideration, not merely a means to human benefit.
- Botany ↔ Mental Health concerns an apparent connection through horticultural therapy and direct plant interaction. However, the reviewed clinical trials largely evaluate therapeutic horticultural activities and participant outcomes, not specific botanical variables such as plant taxonomy, physiology, chemical ecology, growth characteristics, or species-specific mechanisms. The presence of plants in an intervention is not, by itself, proof of a broad Botany ↔ Mental Health disciplinary relationship.

The evidence has important limitations. A 2026 scoping review mapped 28 quantitative studies of biodiversity-related exposures and mental-health outcomes. Associations were generally favourable for psychological well-being, positive affect, restoration, and stress-related outcomes, with some studies reporting fewer depressive or anxiety symptoms. However, most studies were observational, measures were heterogeneous, and none used formal psychiatric diagnostic assessments. A separate 2026 systematic review of urban green-space biodiversity and health included 147 eligible studies; most ecological exposures were vegetation-based indicators, while only a minority used composite ecological indices or fauna-based measures. These sources support a research interface, not a causal clinical claim.

For Zoology ↔ Mental Health, a 2026 meta-analysis included 35 randomized controlled trials and 2,391 adults, reporting post-intervention reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress, but with substantial between-study variability. A contemporary review of animal-assisted interventions examines animal cognition, sensory perception, species choice, behaviour, and welfare; it describes stress signals and the need for animals to be able to withdraw from overwhelming settings. The candidate interface is therefore not merely the use of animals as therapeutic objects: animal biology, behaviour, and welfare are relevant to the design and ethical operation of interventions. The evidence does not establish that animal-assisted interventions are appropriate for every patient, animal, diagnosis, or setting.

For Botany ↔ Mental Health, a 2025 meta-analysis included 13 horticultural-therapy RCTs involving 960 participants and reported improvements across several outcomes, but rated certainty from very low to low because of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision. A 2026 review of horticultural therapy and urban agriculture also described promising findings while noting methodological heterogeneity and moderate risk of bias. These findings support further assessment of horticultural and nature-based interventions. They do not, on their own, establish that botanical science is a distinct contributor to clinical mental-health care. A future reassessment could be justified by research that measures plant species, botanical traits, plant physiological or chemical properties, or other explicit botanical variables and links them to defined mental-health outcomes.

The canonical taxonomy remains the authority for subject names, identifiers, and hierarchy. The inventory contains 18 Life Sciences subjects and 15 Health Sciences subjects, producing 270 possible cross-domain pairs. The preceding assessment snapshots v0.1.0 through v0.39.0 record 173 unique screened pairs, 156 candidate recommendations, and 17 screened but not promoted or deferred, leaving 97 individually unassessed pairs before this batch.
