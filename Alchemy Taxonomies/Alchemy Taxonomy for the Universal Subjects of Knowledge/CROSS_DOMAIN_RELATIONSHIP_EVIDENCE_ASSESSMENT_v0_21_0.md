## 2. Method and terminology boundaries

Each pair is assessed for its own contribution to epidemiological investigation, measurement, and interpretation. The three interfaces are not treated as interchangeable.

- **Ecology ↔ Epidemiology** is assessed through disease ecology and landscape epidemiology. Ecology contributes species interactions, host–pathogen–environment relationships, biodiversity, land use, climate, habitat and landscape processes. Epidemiology contributes defined populations, case definitions, incidence and prevalence measurement, risk-factor estimation, spatial-temporal analysis, surveillance, and outbreak investigation.
- **Disease ecology is an interface, not a merger of the two subjects.** Ecological mechanisms can influence infection, transmission, persistence, and spillover; epidemiological observation and models quantify health events in populations. The strength and direction of ecological drivers vary between pathogen systems, landscapes, and social contexts.
- **Zoology ↔ Epidemiology** is assessed through wildlife and animal-host population epidemiology. Zoology contributes species identity, host biology, distribution, behaviour, population structure, and species interactions. Epidemiology contributes sampling design, population definitions, case definitions, prevalence/incidence measures, risk-factor analysis, and spatial-temporal disease surveillance.
- **Wildlife disease epidemiology is not identical to Zoology ↔ Public Health.** The previously screened Zoology ↔ Public Health relationship concerns the use of animal and wildlife knowledge in population-health threat recognition and surveillance systems. The current pair focuses on epidemiological methods applied to disease occurrence and distribution within defined animal host populations, including how species, sampling units, and population representativeness affect inference. Findings may inform public health, conservation, or veterinary decision-making, but those applications do not define the entirety of the pair.
- **Marine Biology ↔ Epidemiology** is assessed through epidemiological investigation of marine-origin infections and marine organism/toxin-associated illness, with particular attention to coastal Vibrio infection and marine harmful algal blooms. Marine Biology contributes knowledge of marine species, plankton and algae, host ecology, aquatic microbial habitats, marine food webs, and environmental conditions such as temperature and salinity. Epidemiology contributes population case ascertainment, incidence, risk-factor analysis, exposure histories, geographic and seasonal comparisons, and surveillance.
- **Marine Biology is not interchangeable with Microbiology.** Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-003) already covers organism-level microbiological knowledge and population infectious-disease investigation. This new pair addresses the marine ecosystem, marine host and organism context, and oceanographic/ecological determinants of human and animal health events. Microorganism identification is relevant in marine disease cases, but it does not by itself establish the distinct marine-biology contribution.
- **Marine Biology ↔ Epidemiology is also distinct from Marine Biology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-061).** The earlier Public Health assessment considered marine biological knowledge in public-health programmes and surveillance/response. The new assessment focuses specifically on population-level disease occurrence, epidemiological exposure data, risk estimation, and ecological correlates of cases.
- Marine environmental association is not automatic proof of causal influence. Temperature, salinity, phytoplankton, biodiversity, or land-use correlations may be confounded by reporting, healthcare access, exposure behaviour, sampling, and other variables.
- Case reports and surveillance counts do not necessarily represent incidence in all people, all animal populations, or all marine ecosystems. Wildlife is difficult to monitor; passive reports and diagnostic submissions have uneven coverage.
- A candidate RD-P is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. Evidence of a bounded interface does not establish universal dependency, containment, hierarchy, or a universal disease mechanism.

## 4. Duplicate, direction, and scope cross-check

The three selected pairs were checked as unordered subject pairs against all assessment tables in v0.1.0 through v0.20.0. The previous ledger contains 116 unique screened pairs with identifiers LSHS-001 through LSHS-116. Historical name-only entries and later stable-ID entries were both included. None of the selected pairs is an exact duplicate.

Previously assessed and related pairings remain distinct:
- Ecology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-007): an ecosystem-health / population-health policy and surveillance interface, not the current ecological determinants / epidemiological analysis pairing.
- Ecology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-058), Ecology ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-059), Ecology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-067), and Ecology ↔ Nursing (LSHS-074): these concern clinical, prevention, nutritional, or nursing practice applications of ecological knowledge, not the present population-level epidemiological interface.
- Ecology ↔ Emergency Medicine (LSHS-073): not promoted for lack of a sufficiently direct Ecology-specific contribution to emergency clinical assessment or treatment. It is not the same question as disease ecology and epidemiological analysis.
- Evolutionary Biology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-089), Genetics ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-091), Molecular Biology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-093), and Mycology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-111): each concerns distinct evolutionary, inherited-variation, molecular, or fungal-organism contributions to epidemiological questions. None substitutes for ecosystem ecology.
- Zoology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-056): wildlife/animal knowledge and zoonotic public-health surveillance; the current pair addresses epidemiological measurement and inference in animal host populations.
- Zoology ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-057), Zoology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-055), Zoology ↔ Emergency Medicine (LSHS-068), Zoology ↔ Nursing (LSHS-069), and Zoology ↔ rehabilitation disciplines (LSHS-075–077): these involve different clinical, preventive, or rehabilitative applications.
- Marine Biology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-060), Marine Biology ↔ Public Health (LSHS-061), Marine Biology ↔ Preventive Medicine (LSHS-062), and Marine Biology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-066): these concern clinical, public-health, preventive, and nutrition applications. The new pair specifically concerns epidemiological incidence, case distribution, exposure and environmental risk analysis.
- Microbiology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-003) and Virology ↔ Epidemiology (LSHS-004): these concern microbiological/viral agent knowledge. The present Marine Biology pair focuses on marine habitats, organisms and ecosystem variables as context for marine-origin illness.

Direction is reciprocal within each bounded interface:
- LSHS-117: Ecology provides organism–environment mechanisms and landscape context; Epidemiology quantifies health-event distribution, risk factors and population-level outcomes. Epidemiological findings can identify ecological processes that merit further study.
- LSHS-118: Zoology supplies species identity, host biology, animal population structure and distribution; Epidemiology applies defined population, sampling, case and statistical methods. Epidemiological patterns can inform zoological and wildlife-health questions.
- LSHS-119: Marine Biology supplies marine organism, habitat, plankton/algal and environmental context; Epidemiology estimates the distribution and determinants of marine-origin illness across human and animal populations. Epidemiological findings can refine marine monitoring priorities.

None of these proposals is a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim.
