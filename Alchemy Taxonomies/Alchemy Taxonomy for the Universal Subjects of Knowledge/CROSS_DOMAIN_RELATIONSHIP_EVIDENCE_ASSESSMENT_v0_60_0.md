## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. The selected subgroup examines Life Sciences in relation to Public Health, Epidemiology, and Nutrition. The central test is whether the full Life Sciences subject contributes an integrated, multi-level body of biological evidence or method that cannot be represented adequately by one previously assessed specialist discipline.

The assessment distinguishes:

- **Life Sciences ↔ Public Health:** molecular public health, precision public health, and exposome science demonstrate a developing operational interface in which biological evidence is combined across pathogens, hosts, microbial communities, molecular profiles, and biological exposures, then interpreted with environmental and population data to support surveillance, risk assessment, prevention, intervention, and evaluation. A 2026 review defines molecular public health as an end-to-end framework connecting molecular signals with population-level decisions and interventions. It integrates molecular detection, pathogen genomics, bioinformatics, metagenomics, wastewater and environmental surveillance, One Health monitoring, antimicrobial-resistance genomics, multi-omics, and AI. A separate precision-public-health review connects genetics, lifestyle, environment, human and pathogen genomics, and social and behavioral information with population-level intervention targeting. Exposome research adds integrated measurement of chemical, physical, and biological exposures, including the microbial exposome, and seeks to translate molecular precision into population-scale prevention. The interface is therefore broader than any one previously screened specialist pairing: it concerns integration of multi-level life-science evidence into a public-health decision cycle. The evidence is emerging and implementation maturity is heterogeneous; some targeted molecular methods are established in defined settings, while pathogen-agnostic metagenomics, multi-omics, and AI-supported decision tools remain context-dependent or developing. This candidate does not imply that biological measurements alone determine public-health policy or that technology adoption itself improves health outcomes. [S550–S553]
- **Life Sciences ↔ Epidemiology:** large-scale population cohorts increasingly integrate deep molecular profiling, multi-omics, imaging, biomarkers, phenomics, digital physiology, electronic health records, and longitudinal outcomes. This integration moves some cohort research beyond descriptive associations toward investigation of mechanisms, risk stratification, disease trajectories, and hypotheses about biological pathways in population contexts. A 2026 review describes the transformation of population cohorts into large biobanks with multi-omics and deep phenotyping. A 2026 systematic review in the *International Journal of Epidemiology* identified 147 contemporary longitudinal cardiovascular cohorts and documented increased integration of multimodal imaging, genetics, biomarker profiling, and digital health. A large prospective resource, Our Future Health, reported baseline phenotypic data for more than 1.9 million participants and compared disease patterns with national estimates and the UK Biobank. This is a systems-level interface: epidemiology supplies sampling, longitudinal design, population comparison, confounding control, and disease-pattern inference; Life Sciences supplies integrated biological measurements and mechanistic interpretations across multiple levels. It is distinct from the previously assessed one-specialist-at-a-time pairs such as Genetics ↔ Epidemiology or Molecular Biology ↔ Epidemiology. Important limitations include non-representativeness, incomplete phenotypic depth, heterogeneity in follow-up and measurement, and the fact that observational associations do not establish causation. The Our Future Health article's 26 October 2026 publisher correction is noted in the source register; the report relies on the corrected version. [S554–S557]
- **Life Sciences ↔ Nutrition:** precision nutrition increasingly uses integrated genomic, epigenetic, microbiome, metabolomic, and physiological evidence to characterize inter-individual differences in response to dietary exposures. A 2026 review of fluxomics explains how stable-isotope tracing, high-resolution mass spectrometry, NMR, metabolomics, and computational flux maps can capture dynamic metabolic responses that static dietary biomarkers may miss. A separate 2026 review discusses multi-omics, dynamic phenotyping, functional-food heterogeneity, and AI-supported decisions, while explicitly noting that the incremental clinical value of additional molecular data remains uncertain. A 2026 review of the molecular basis of precision nutrition connects food components and microbial metabolites—including short-chain fatty acids and bile-acid pathways—with host gene regulation and metabolic phenotypes. A 2025 Nature Medicine review describes deep phenotyping and individual variation in dietary response, alongside the challenges of translating proof-of-concept findings into broader implementation. This is an integrative systems interface across biological levels and response dynamics, not simply another single-specialist relationship such as Genetics ↔ Nutrition, Molecular Biology ↔ Nutrition, Microbiology ↔ Nutrition, or Physiology ↔ Nutrition. It does not imply that multi-omics profiling is necessary for routine nutrition care, that every additional data layer improves decisions, or that molecular changes alone prove health benefit. [S558–S562]

A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. A candidate does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, or verification. The recommendations are bounded to the described integrative interfaces, not universal dependencies between the full subject areas. No canonical change is made by this report.

Direction and scope:

- LSHS-234 is reciprocal at the evidence-to-action level: integrated biological evidence informs population surveillance and preventive decisions; population health priorities, observed patterns, and intervention outcomes guide further life-science investigation.
- LSHS-235 is reciprocal at the population-research level: life-science phenotyping and mechanistic models enrich epidemiological cohort questions and interpretation; epidemiological patterns, associations, and population gaps guide biological inquiry and model refinement.
- LSHS-236 is reciprocal at the systems-nutrition level: multi-level biological findings inform analysis of dietary response and nutritional intervention design; observed dietary exposures and response phenotypes direct new life-science research into metabolism, microbiome, and host regulation.

Important boundaries:

- Molecular and precision public-health frameworks are not uniformly mature. Multi-omics, metagenomics, AI-enabled interpretation, and some integrated host-response approaches remain context-dependent or emerging; decisions require analytical validity, epidemiological interpretability, timeliness, equity, and measurable impact.
- Epidemiological cohorts with extensive biological phenotyping remain unevenly distributed and may under-represent populations by geography, age, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status. Cohort scale does not guarantee population representativeness.
- Multi-omic cohort associations do not establish causation. Exposure measurement, selection bias, confounding, missingness, harmonization, and follow-up differences must be considered.
- Precision nutrition research is not a replacement for population dietary guidance or established nutrition practice. The clinical value of additional molecular profiling remains inconsistent, and dynamic metabolic measures require reproducibility and decision-impact validation.
- The Life Sciences root-level candidates are bounded to integrative multi-level methods; they do not imply that all public-health, epidemiological, or nutritional work requires complex life-science profiling.

None of these decisions creates a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim. This assessment does not convert emerging molecular public health into universal practice, observational cohort findings into causal proof, or precision-nutrition biomarker changes into demonstrated clinical benefit.
