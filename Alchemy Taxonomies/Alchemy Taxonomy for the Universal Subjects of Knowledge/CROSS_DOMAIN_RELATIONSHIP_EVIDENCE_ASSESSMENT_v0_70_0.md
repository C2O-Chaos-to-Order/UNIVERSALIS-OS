A root-level candidate must also explain why its integrated scope adds value beyond its existing specialist pairings without claiming that every Health Sciences activity depends on the Life Sciences subject.

The recommendations are bounded as follows:

- **Biotechnology ↔ Health Sciences:** biotechnology platform, process, and biological-product development that informs multiple health-research and health-service settings, including diagnostics, biologics, vaccine and therapeutic development, biosurveillance, and selected regenerative or genome-editing applications.

Research relevance is not clinical validation. A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. It does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, verification, or owner acceptance. No canonical change is made by this report.

### LSHS-264 — Biotechnology ↔ Health Sciences

Biotechnology contributes the design, modification, engineering, characterization, and production of biological systems, biological materials, and biologically based methods. Health Sciences contributes the clinical and population-health questions, patient and disease contexts, diagnostic and preventive needs, therapeutic indications, safety and effectiveness criteria, regulatory evaluation, and health-system implementation through which those technologies are assessed and used.

The evidence demonstrates a platform-level interface that is broader than any one medical specialty. FDA's October 2026 draft guidance for individualized human genome-editing products addresses how multiple patient-specific product variants may be included in a biologics licence application, as well as product variation, in-vitro safety and potency evaluation, manufacturing-process validation, labelling, and postapproval management. The document is explicitly a draft for comment and is not for implementation; it establishes an emerging regulatory-development interface, not an approved product or demonstrated patient benefit. WHO's September 2026 work on life-science and dual-use governance identifies the need to support biotechnology and genomic innovation while strengthening safety, security, responsible oversight, and risk anticipation. A 2026 review of biotechnology approaches to cancer drug resistance discusses CRISPR/Cas9, targeted therapies, nanotechnology-enabled delivery, immunotherapies, biomarker-guided treatment, and public-health implications; as a review spanning basic, clinical, translational, and policy literature, it supports the existence of a research interface but does not establish uniform effectiveness across technologies or indications. [S645–S647; previously registered S71–S78 and relevant specialist-assessment evidence]

The relationship is distinct from Biotechnology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-024), Pharmacology (LSHS-025), Surgery (LSHS-026), Public Health (LSHS-027), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-028), Nursing (LSHS-099), Dentistry (LSHS-100), Nutrition (LSHS-170), Physical Therapy (LSHS-180), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-181), Occupational Therapy (LSHS-182), Epidemiology (LSHS-196), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-207), and Mental Health (LSHS-214). These records concern specific application or practice settings. The earlier not-promoted Biotechnology ↔ Nursing (LSHS-099) and Biotechnology ↔ Occupational Therapy (LSHS-182) decisions remain not promoted; the broader root-level recommendation does not reverse them or assert that incidental exposure to a biotechnology-derived product establishes a distinct specialty relationship. LSHS-264 instead concerns the cross-cutting platform, process, and biological-product development interface spanning multiple Health Sciences settings.

**Candidate RD-P:** biotechnology design and engineering of biological systems; genetic and cellular modification; recombinant and other biologic platforms; vaccine and therapeutic-platform development; engineered diagnostic and biosensing methods; bioprocessing, biomanufacturing, and biological-product characterization / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and services involving diagnostic and preventive technologies, biologics and vaccine development, genome-editing and cell-based product evaluation, biosurveillance, selected regenerative applications, and assessment and implementation of biological technologies. Biotechnology contributes platform design, biological engineering, production methods, and product characterization; Health Sciences contributes clinical and population-health needs, disease and health phenotypes, diagnostic and therapeutic questions, safety and efficacy standards, regulatory and professional requirements, outcome evidence, and implementation contexts. The relationship is reciprocal: health needs and observed outcomes guide technology design and evaluation, while biotechnology methods and products enable selected health-science research and practice. This does not imply that every biotechnology platform has a health application, that every product is approved or clinically effective, or that any specialist-pair decision is superseded.**

### LSHS-265 — Neuroscience ↔ Health Sciences

Neuroscience contributes knowledge and methods concerning the structure, function, development, organization, and activity of the nervous system, including neural cells and circuits, sensory and motor systems, cognition, behaviour, brain-body interactions, neurophysiology, and neurological mechanisms. Health Sciences contributes the clinical and population-health contexts, disease and functional phenotypes, care pathways, prevention priorities, therapeutic evaluation, and service outcomes through which neuroscience findings are investigated and translated.

NIH's BRAIN Initiative priorities encompass human neuroscience, neural recording and modulation, neuroimaging, systems neuroscience, brain-cell and circuit research, data science, validated tools for basic, translational, and clinical research, and neuroethics. The August 2025 BRAIN Initiative research roadmap describes the need to integrate discovery with human studies and clinical technologies while addressing ethical, legal, societal, privacy, consent, safety, and fairness considerations. A 2026 *Neuron* roadmap describes further integration of basic and human neuroscience toward treatments and preventive interventions. A 2026 interdisciplinary neurotechnology roadmap identifies translation trade-offs across materials, scalability, sensing, computational integration, and clinical or commercial applicability. These sources, together with previously registered WHO neurological-burden and translational-neuroscience sources, support a cross-cutting research and translation interface. They do not establish that every neuroscience discovery will translate into a validated intervention or improved patient outcome. [S648–S650; previously registered S292, S295, and S303–S305]

The relationship is distinct from Neuroscience ↔ Mental Health (LSHS-005), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-032), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-033), Physical Therapy (LSHS-034), Occupational Therapy (LSHS-084), Surgery (LSHS-101), Pharmacology (LSHS-114), Epidemiology (LSHS-129), Public Health (LSHS-130), Preventive Medicine (LSHS-131), Medicine (LSHS-135), Nursing (LSHS-136), Nutrition (LSHS-137), and Dentistry (LSHS-165). Those records address individual clinical, professional, research, or population-health settings. LSHS-265 concerns the broader neuroscience contribution across multiple Health Sciences contexts, not a claim that Neuroscience is equivalent to Mental Health, Neurology, or any single clinical specialty. It does not reverse or aggregate the earlier specialist decisions into a new clinical scope.

**Candidate RD-P:** nervous-system organization and function; neural cells, circuits, connectivity, and signalling; sensory, motor, cognitive, and behavioural neuroscience; neurodevelopment and neural plasticity; neurophysiology and brain-body mechanisms; human and comparative neuroscience methods; and neural measurement and modulation technologies / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and practice involving neurological and neuropsychiatric investigation, brain-health research, diagnostic and monitoring development, prevention research, therapeutic and neurotechnology development, functional recovery research, and evaluation of nervous-system contributions to health and disease. Neuroscience contributes nervous-system mechanisms, measurements, models, and explanatory frameworks; Health Sciences contributes patient and population contexts, clinical and functional questions, care and prevention pathways, safety and efficacy evaluation, and outcome evidence. The relationship is reciprocal: health questions and outcomes shape neuroscience research, while validated neuroscience findings inform selected health-science research and applications. This does not imply that every mental-health or health condition is primarily neurological, that animal or computational findings alone establish human clinical effectiveness, or that every neuroscience method is suitable for patient care.**

### LSHS-266 — Physiology ↔ Health Sciences

Physiology contributes knowledge of how living systems function and regulate themselves across cellular, tissue, organ, and whole-organism scales, including homeostasis, feedback, adaptation, integration, and interactions among organ systems. Health Sciences contributes clinical and population-health questions, functional and disease phenotypes, assessment and monitoring contexts, intervention and prevention goals, and patient or population outcomes through which physiological knowledge is evaluated and applied.

NIH's September 2025 whole-person health initiative explicitly aims to develop an integrated knowledge network of healthy physiological function and to study interconnected cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal, and other functions rather than treating organ systems in isolation. The *Frontiers in Physiology* Clinical and Translational Physiology section defines its scope around integrating basic physiology and related research with clinical research and clinical practice. A 2026 *Annual Review of Physiology* review describes digital systems physiology as linking measurements with mechanistic models and multi-organ feedback, adaptation, and coupling. Its authors distinguish mechanistic insight, individualized simulation, and early clinical feasibility from demonstrated improvement in patient outcomes. These sources demonstrate an integrative research and measurement interface while supporting a restrained claim about clinical maturity. [S651–S653; previously registered S93–S98, S221, and S253–S255]

The relationship is distinct from Physiology ↔ Medicine (LSHS-001), Nutrition (LSHS-006), Emergency Medicine (LSHS-037), Rehabilitation Science (LSHS-038), Physical Therapy (LSHS-039), Occupational Therapy (LSHS-085), Surgery (LSHS-102), Pharmacology (LSHS-115), Nursing (LSHS-144), Public Health (LSHS-145), Epidemiology (LSHS-146), Dentistry (LSHS-166), Mental Health (LSHS-177), and Preventive Medicine (LSHS-185). These records concern specific physiological applications in individual Health Sciences settings, including acute assessment, perioperative management, exercise and rehabilitation, nutrition, monitoring, environmental risk, and preventive cardiometabolic assessment. LSHS-266 concerns the cross-cutting role of physiological function, regulation, measurement, and systems integration across multiple Health Sciences contexts. It does not claim that every health-science decision requires physiological modelling or that a measured physiological variable is automatically a validated surrogate endpoint or treatment-selection tool.

**Candidate RD-P:** cellular and organ-system function; homeostasis and physiological regulation; feedback, adaptation, and stress responses; cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, metabolic, neuroendocrine, musculoskeletal, and integrative physiology; physiological measurement and functional assessment; and mechanistic or computational systems-physiology models / cross-disciplinary Health Sciences research and practice involving health and disease mechanisms, functional assessment, physiological monitoring, risk and response evaluation, exercise and rehabilitation research, preventive and therapeutic intervention assessment, and selected integrative or computational modelling. Physiology contributes functional mechanisms, systems-level explanations, measurement principles, and models; Health Sciences contributes clinical and population-health questions, relevant functional and disease phenotypes, assessment and monitoring contexts, intervention decisions, and outcome validation. The relationship is reciprocal: health-science questions and observations guide physiological investigation, while physiological knowledge informs selected research, assessment, and evaluation. This does not assert that all health services depend on advanced physiological tests, that mechanistic models prove clinical benefit, or that specialist-pair assessments are replaced.**
