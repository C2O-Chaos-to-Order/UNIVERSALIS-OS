## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. It assesses three previously unassessed interfaces within nutrition, food, and microbial systems:

- Microbiology ↔ Nutrition: dietary patterns, gut microbial ecology and metabolism, microbial nutrient synthesis, fermented foods, and effects of microbial activity on nutrient availability and absorption.
- Mycology ↔ Nutrition: edible fungal fruiting bodies and mycelia, mycoprotein, fungal fermentation, nutritional composition and protein quality, digestibility, bioavailability, and fungal food safety.
- Biotechnology ↔ Nutrition: precision fermentation, engineered microbial cell factories, metabolic engineering, and production of food proteins, enzymes, flavors, and micronutrients, evaluated in relation to nutritional composition, completeness, digestibility, bioavailability, and dietary use.

These recommendations are related but distinct:

- Microbiology ↔ Nutrition addresses organismal and community-level microbial processes that interact with diet and nutrient metabolism. It includes the influence of dietary substrates on microbial communities and the contribution of microbial metabolism to nutrient synthesis, transformation, and availability.
- Mycology ↔ Nutrition focuses on fungi as food organisms and food-production organisms: fungal fruiting bodies, mycelial biomass, mycoprotein, fungal fermentation, and fungal-specific nutritional and safety characteristics. It does not simply repeat the broader microbial-community and diet interface.
- Biotechnology ↔ Nutrition focuses on engineered technological platforms—such as precision fermentation, engineered microbial cell factories, and metabolic pathway design—that produce or modify food ingredients. It is not limited to fungi or to naturally occurring microbial ecology.

The source base supports all three as substantive research interfaces, but with important limits. A 2026 systematic review of 80 controlled dietary-intervention trials found that dietary interventions were associated with changes in gut microbial taxa and selected biomarkers, but not consistently with changes in alpha or beta diversity; it also reported substantial heterogeneity in methods and reporting. Microbial vitamin production and phytate degradation can affect nutrient availability, but strain, food matrix, fermentation conditions, stability, and regulatory requirements matter. Fungal foods and mycoproteins have species- and process-dependent nutritional properties, and allergenicity, toxicity, digestibility, and food-safety questions remain active research areas. Precision-fermented and engineered food ingredients require independent evaluation of nutritional completeness, composition, bioavailability, safety, regulatory status, and consumer acceptance. These interfaces therefore do not establish that a particular diet, probiotic, fungal product, or engineered food produces a universal clinical benefit.

The canonical taxonomy remains the authority for subject names, identifiers, and hierarchy. The inventory contains 18 Life Sciences subjects and 15 Health Sciences subjects, producing 270 possible cross-domain pairs. The preceding assessment snapshots v0.1.0 through v0.37.0 record 167 unique screened pairs, 151 candidate recommendations, and 16 screened but not promoted or deferred, leaving 103 individually unassessed pairs before this batch.

Each selected pair was checked as an unordered subject pair against all 37 prior assessment snapshots, including early name-only rows and later stable-ID rows. Historical assessment IDs LSHS-001 through LSHS-167 were reconciled. None of the three selected pairs appears in the prior ledger.

A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. A candidate does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, or verification. No canonical change is made by this report.

## 3. Duplicate, distinction, and scope cross-check

The three selected pairs were checked as unordered subject pairs against all 37 prior assessment snapshots, v0.1.0 through v0.37.0. The reconciled prior ledger contains 167 unique screened pairs with assessment IDs LSHS-001 through LSHS-167. Checks were insensitive to subject order and covered both name-only historical rows and stable-ID rows. No occurrence of Microbiology ↔ Nutrition, Mycology ↔ Nutrition, or Biotechnology ↔ Nutrition was found in the prior ledger. Canonical subject names and identifiers were checked against taxonomy v1.13.1.

Previously assessed and related pairs remain distinct:

- Nutrition ↔ Physiology (LSHS-006) concerns physiological processes and nutrition. It is not the microbial-community, microbial-metabolism, and diet interface assessed in LSHS-168.
- Botany ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-063), Marine Biology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-066), and Ecology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-067) concern botanical, marine, and ecological interfaces with nutrition. They do not replace the distinct organismal scope of Microbiology ↔ Nutrition or Mycology ↔ Nutrition.
- Immunology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-106), Genetics ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-123), Molecular Biology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-124), Cell Biology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-125), Neuroscience ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-137), and Virology ↔ Nutrition (LSHS-152) concern immune, genetic, molecular, cellular, neural, and viral interfaces with nutrition. None is an exact duplicate of the broad microbial ecology and metabolism interface in LSHS-168, the fungal food-organism interface in LSHS-169, or the engineered food-production platform interface in LSHS-170.
- Existing Microbiology relationships with Medicine, Nursing, Surgery, Public Health, Emergency Medicine, Preventive Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacology, and Mental Health concern other Health Sciences applications. LSHS-168 is specifically the interaction of microbes, diet, nutrient metabolism, and nutritional availability.
- Existing Mycology relationships with Medicine, Dentistry, Emergency Medicine, Nursing, Preventive Medicine, Pharmacology, Epidemiology, Public Health, Surgery, and Mental Health concern other Health Sciences applications. LSHS-169 is specifically about fungi as food and fermentation organisms and their nutritional composition, quality, and safety.
- Existing Biotechnology relationships with Medicine, Pharmacology, Surgery, Public Health, Preventive Medicine, Nursing, and Dentistry concern other applications. LSHS-170 is specifically about biological engineering and production platforms for food ingredients and their nutritional evaluation.
- Mycology ↔ Nutrition and Microbiology ↔ Nutrition are not collapsed into one pair. LSHS-168 focuses on microbial communities, metabolism, and diet-associated nutrient interactions; LSHS-169 focuses on fungal organisms, fungal biomass, mycoprotein, and fungal food production. Biological overlap does not erase subject distinction.
- Biotechnology ↔ Nutrition is not a rewording of either organism-specific pair. LSHS-170 focuses on engineered platforms and process design across potential biological hosts, while LSHS-168 and LSHS-169 focus on knowledge of microbial and fungal organisms and their metabolism.

Direction and scope:

- LSHS-168 is reciprocal: Microbiology contributes microbial community and metabolic knowledge; Nutrition frames dietary substrates and evaluates nutrient availability, adequacy, and health relevance. Diet and food composition also affect microbial growth and activity.
- LSHS-169 is reciprocal: Mycology contributes fungal species, strain, growth, and food-production knowledge; Nutrition evaluates the resulting food's composition, quality, digestibility, bioavailability, safety, and dietary use. Nutritional targets can inform fungal food research and cultivation.
- LSHS-170 is reciprocal: Biotechnology contributes engineered systems and production methods; Nutrition defines nutritional targets and evaluates the resulting ingredients and foods. Nutritional assessment can inform process and product design.

None of these recommendations creates a hierarchy edge, universal dependency, or enablement claim. The assessment does not imply that probiotics or microbiome changes guarantee health improvement, that all fungal species or products are safe or nutritionally complete, or that precision-fermented and engineered foods are inherently healthier.
