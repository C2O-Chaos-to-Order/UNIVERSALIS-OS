## 1. Scope and safeguards

This report continues the Life Sciences ↔ Health Sciences cross-domain screening programme. The selected subgroup examines three distinct interfaces: biotechnology-enabled point-of-care diagnostics in emergency medicine; cellular effect biomarkers used in population environmental-health monitoring; and ecological or environmental characteristics of operating rooms in relation to surgery.

The assessment distinguishes:

- **Biotechnology ↔ Emergency Medicine:** biotechnology-enabled near-patient diagnostic systems, including molecular amplification, CRISPR-based recognition, integrated biosensors, microfluidic sample handling, cartridge systems, and connected readouts, in relation to time-critical emergency assessment and triage. Biotechnology contributes biological assay design and integrated diagnostic platforms; Emergency Medicine contributes acute-care questions, decision windows, triage, and evaluation within emergency workflows. The evidence supports a technology-to-workflow interface, but does not establish that every prototype is clinically validated or that faster testing consistently improves major patient outcomes.
- **Cell Biology ↔ Public Health:** cellular effect biomarkers—including DNA strand breaks in leukocytes, cell-level responses to environmental and occupational exposures, and cellular damage endpoints—used in human biomonitoring and population environmental-health surveillance. Cell Biology contributes the mechanisms, structures, and cellular measurement methods; Public Health contributes population exposure assessment, community surveillance, risk assessment, environmental justice, and prevention-oriented interpretation. The evidence does not make a cellular biomarker a stand-alone diagnosis, a direct measure of disease burden, or proof that an exposure caused an individual outcome.
- **Ecology ↔ Surgery:** ecological and environmental questions surrounding operating-room airborne microbiota, particulate matter, ventilation, and surgical-site infection risk. Recent studies do demonstrate environmental and microbial dynamics during surgical procedures. However, the reviewed work is chiefly framed as surgical infection prevention, environmental microbiology, occupational exposure, operating-room engineering, and life-cycle assessment. It does not yet establish a sufficiently distinct and sustained contribution from the discipline of Ecology to Surgery beyond the adjacent environmental and microbiological methods. This pair is not promoted.

A candidate RD-P recommendation is a proposal for owner review, not an accepted or registered canonical edge. A candidate does not establish hierarchy, dependency, enablement, implementation, testing, or verification. A non-promotion is specific to the scope and evidence assessed in this pass; it does not prove that no narrower future interface could be supported. No canonical change is made by this report.
